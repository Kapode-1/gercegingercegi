Three Jurisdictions in the Njeem Case: Can Libya Take the Case Away from the ICC?

YAYIN ODAPTARİH 02.11.2026BÖLGE LİBYA / ICCDİL EN
YAZARTayyip Minsin
SERİODAP Analizleri
STATÜODAP'ta yayımlandı
DOSYATM–ODAP/007

On November 2, 2026, the Pre-Trial Chamber of the International Criminal Court unanimously confirmed that the Court had jurisdiction over the case against Osama Elmasry Njeem. However, the Chamber deferred its decision on the admissibility of the case. Njeem’s defence argued that the alleged conduct fell outside the scope of United Nations Security Council Resolution 1970 and that relevant domestic proceedings were under way in Libya. The Chamber rejected the jurisdictional challenge but gave the defence and the Libyan authorities until January 29, 2027, to submit additional information. The future of the case will therefore depend on whether the proceedings in Libya constitute a genuine accountability process.

This distinction arises from the principle of complementarity under the Rome Statute. The ICC does not automatically replace national courts. If a state is genuinely investigating the same person for substantially the same conduct, priority may be given to its national judicial system. It will therefore not be enough for Libya merely to state that a case has been opened against Njeem. The domestic proceedings must cover the allegations contained in the ICC arrest warrant, including alleged crimes against humanity and war crimes such as murder, torture, rape and sexual violence. The investigation must also make tangible progress and remain independent of political interference. If Libya satisfies these requirements, the case could be declared inadmissible before the ICC. If the domestic proceedings are found to be merely formal or intended to obstruct international prosecution, the ICC may continue with the case.

The second dimension concerns Njeem’s arrest in Italy and subsequent return to Libya. The ICC issued its arrest warrant on January 18, 2025, and Njeem was detained in Turin the following day. He was released two days later because the domestic procedure required for his surrender to the ICC had not been properly followed. He was then returned to Libya on an Italian state aircraft. The ICC later found that Italy had failed to comply with its cooperation obligations under the Rome Statute. The episode demonstrated the extent to which international courts, which lack their own law enforcement bodies, depend on states to execute arrest warrants.

The third jurisdictional dimension involves the applications Y v. Italy and Z v. Italy before the European Court of Human Rights. The Court has not ruled that Italy committed a human rights violation. It has communicated the applications to the Italian government and requested its observations. The issue under examination is not Njeem’s criminal responsibility, but whether Italy’s failure to complete the surrender process affected the applicants’ rights to an effective investigation and access to accountability. The central challenge in the Njeem case is therefore not simply which court has jurisdiction. It is whether Libya can conduct a genuine national prosecution and whether states are willing to cooperate effectively with international institutions when confronted with allegations of serious international crimes.
